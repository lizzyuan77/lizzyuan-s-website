18 museums · 8 cities · September 21, 2026 price snapshot

What does the card actually save?

You have chosen a museum. Is it worth finding your student ID before leaving home? To answer that practical question, I used R’s rvest package to collect official admission information for 18 museums across eight U.S. cities. The comparison holds the visit and visitor’s age constant: what does a student pay, compared with an otherwise equivalent nonstudent?

That distinction matters. A museum that admits everyone free offers no extra student discount. A youth ticket available to nonstudents is not a benefit of enrollment. I therefore measure student-ID saving as the adult price minus the student price at the same age.

The baseline is a 30-year-old, nonresident, full-time college student with valid ID. Age 30 separates student eligibility from youth offers; the explorer also compares 22-year-olds. School partnerships, residency benefits, memberships, fees, parking, and separately ticketed exhibitions are excluded.

Where forgetting your ID costs more

Philadelphia’s average saving is $12.75 per sampled museum, compared with $9.50 in New York. This is a comparison of selected institutions, not evidence that Philadelphia is the best city for every student: the sample includes four Philadelphia museums but only two in New York, and some cities include free museums.

The individual prices are more useful for a decision. At the Philadelphia Museum of Art and PAFA, the recorded student tickets total $24.00, versus $55.00 at adult rates. If you already plan to visit both on regular admission days, using your student ID saves $31.00. This is a two-visit budget, not a suggested one-day itinerary.

At Woodmere, the snapshot lists $0.00 for students versus $15.00 for adults. Here, the card eliminates the general-admission charge. On its recorded free Sundays, however, both groups enter free: the additional student saving is zero. The discount matters on an ordinary paid day, not equally on every visit.

Same museum, different age, different benefit

At the Whitney, the baseline student price is $24.00 rather than $30.00: a $6.00 student discount. But visitors 25 and under qualified for free admission regardless of enrollment. For a 22-year-old, the comparison becomes zero against zero. Being a student contributes no additional saving there.

Switching both groups to age 22 reduces New York’s mean student-ID saving from $9.50 to $6.50. That does not mean young students pay more; it means part of their benefit comes from age rather than student status. The Frick’s recorded student discount, $13.00, remains relevant in that scenario.

Before your next museum visit

Bring your ID when the regular student rate is below the rate you otherwise qualify for. For the PMA–PAFA pair, this snapshot puts that benefit at $31.00. At Woodmere on a paid day it is $15.00; at the Whitney for a 22-year-old it is zero. These are specific savings on the same visits, rather than reasons to travel to a different city.

Use the explorer to select your city and age, compare the “Saving” column, and check the official eligibility link before buying. Free hours and pay-what-you-can options may lower both prices; MCA Chicago’s displayed online rates are not mandatory in-person charges. These are historical observations, not current ticket quotes.

How to check and reproduce the results

I screened 30 institutions, retaining 18 with comparable, extractable prices. rvest parses cached official price text; reviewed patterns identify ticket categories. Required categories must be present, and missing prices never become zeros. Requests were spaced and cached; access failures and restrictive terms led to exclusions. This convenience sample, mostly art museums, cannot establish representative city rankings.

File guide and replication instructions explain the source list, exclusions, and limits of the short evidence extracts. One offline R command regenerates the CSVs, figures, and explorer from that evidence.
