What Can a Campus Poll Really Tell Us?

Learn statistical inference by drawing, resampling, and testing fictional Penn polls

simulation
statistics
public policy
Four hands-on simulations use a fictional Penn funding poll to explain sampling uncertainty, bootstrap intervals, coverage, and hypothesis testing.
Author

Jiaqi Yuan

Published

October 28, 2026

The Penn Fund helps support financial aid, teaching, and student life. Penn First Plus supports first-generation and lower-income students. Imagine asking students: should first-generation support be the top priority for an additional alumni-funded gift?

Our fictional poll finds 283 out of 500 students—56.6%—say yes. Everyone choosing another priority counts as no. Does this sample give us good evidence that most students agree?

These are made-up responses, not real Penn votes. We set support in the fictional population at 55%. Knowing that answer lets us check whether our statistical tools work.

1. How uncertain is one poll?

A sample rarely matches the whole population exactly. We need a way to show how uncertain its answer is.

The bootstrap starts with the responses we already have. Imagine writing each answer on a card and putting all 500 cards in a bag. Draw one, record its answer, put it back, and draw again. Stop after 500 draws. Some cards appear more than once; others never appear. This is one resample.

Click Step 1: Draw the reference sample, then Add 100. Each bar shows how often a percentage appears across the resamples. Complete 4,000 to build the full bootstrap distribution.

To make a 95% confidence interval, remove the lowest 2.5% and highest 2.5% of results. The middle 95% runs from 52.4% to 61.0%. This range expresses uncertainty around our sample’s answer.

Try 99% confidence: the range gets wider because we keep more results. More resamples make the endpoints steadier; they do not add new students.

2. What does “95% confidence” mean?

Now start over with new students each time. For every new poll, build a new bootstrap interval. Because we know the true answer is 55%, we can check whether each interval includes it.

Each horizontal line below is one interval. Purple lines include 55%; orange lines miss it. The vertical line marks the fixed truth.

Click Complete 2,000. At sample size 500, 1,923 of 2,000 intervals include 55%: 96.2%. That is close to 95%, though not exact. Our method is approximate, responses come in whole-person counts, and simulation results vary.

“95% confidence” describes what the method does across many new samples: about 95 out of 100 intervals should include the truth when the method works well. It does not mean that 95% of students fall inside the range. Compare 90% and 99%: wider ranges usually miss less often.

3. Could a 50–50 population give us this poll?

Suppose students are evenly divided. This starting claim is the null hypothesis. Simulate polls under that claim and see how often they look at least as unusual as ours.

Our poll’s 56.6% is 6.6 percentage points from 50%. A result the same distance below 50% is equally unusual. We use that distance as our test statistic. The graph combines departures in both directions into one right-hand tail.

Click Complete 100,000. Of those simulated polls, 349 are at least as far from 50% as ours. The p-value is 349 ÷ 100,000, or about 0.0035. In other words, a truly tied population rarely produces a poll this far from a tie.

The shaded rejection region marks results we decided beforehand would count against a tie. Its rule wrongly rejects a true tie no more than 5% of the time. Our 6.6-point distance exceeds its 4.6-point threshold, so we reject the tie at the 5% level.

A p-value is not the probability that the tie is true, as the American Statistical Association explains.

Extra: Why do polls disagree?

Draw several new polls below. Their answers change even though the population stays the same. Increase the sample size: results generally cluster more tightly around 55%. More people usually give a more precise poll.

What should we learn?

One poll gives an estimate. A confidence interval shows its uncertainty; a hypothesis test checks how surprising it would be under a stated claim. Neither tells us which funding choice improves students’ lives. These examples assume fair, independent sampling. More simulations cannot fix a poll that leaves some students out.
