What Can a Campus Poll Really Tell Us?

Learn statistical inference by drawing, resampling, and testing fictional Penn polls

simulation
statistics
public policy
Four hands-on simulations use a fictional Penn funding poll to explain sampling uncertainty, bootstrap intervals, coverage, and hypothesis testing.
Author

Jiaqi Yuan

Published

October 28, 2026

The Penn Fund supports undergraduate financial aid, academics, technology, and student organizations. Penn First Plus serves first-generation and lower-income students. These real priorities motivate a fictional question: would most undergraduates put first-generation support first when choosing an additional alumni-funded gift’s use?

Our example poll records 283 of 500 choices—56.6%—for first-generation support, combining all other priorities into one category. These are simulated responses, not official Penn votes. In our invented population, true support is 55%. Knowing the truth lets us investigate how inference works: learning about a population from an imperfect sample.

Four experiments you can build yourself

Use Draw one, Add, or Play batches to watch each distribution develop. Complete reveals the full run; Start over replays it. Draws follow fixed seeds for reproducibility. Each sample-size setting has its own independently drawn reference poll.

Open the demos separately · Download the reference summary

Demo 1: Estimate uncertainty when the truth is unknown

A real researcher cannot repeatedly survey the entire population. The bootstrap instead uses one observed sample as a stand-in.

Start with Sample → bootstrap, then click Draw the reference sample. Imagine putting the reference poll’s response cards in a bag. Draw a card, record it, put it back, and continue until the resample has 500 answers. The repeated-card display shows why one person can appear several times.

Add 100 resamples, then complete 4,000. Their percentages form a bootstrap distribution. Its 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles bound our 95% interval: 52.4%–61.0%. Switch from 90% to 99% confidence: keeping more of the distribution generally makes the interval wider. More resamples improve calculation stability; they do not create new respondents.

Demo 2: Does a “95% interval” catch the truth?

Now repeat the whole procedure: draw a new poll, bootstrap it 4,000 times, and check its interval against the known 55%. Reveal intervals individually or play batches. Purple intervals contain the truth; orange ones miss. The truth stays fixed while intervals move.

With only ten polls, the running success rate can jump around. Complete 2,000: at sample size 500, 1,923 intervals contain 55%—96.2%. Compare 90%, 95%, and 99% methods. Wider intervals generally catch the truth more often.

This repeated-sample performance is what confidence describes. It is not a probability that the fixed truth moves inside a particular finished interval. Coverage is approximate; discrete responses and simulation variability help explain why our result is not exactly 95%.

Demo 3: Could a tied population produce this result?

Switch to Test a tie. Change the assumed population to 50% choosing first-generation support. This is the null hypothesis, a reference claim—not something we already know is true.

Simulate polls under that claim. Count those at least as far from 50% as the reference poll, in either direction. The running fraction—extreme polls divided by all null polls—is the two-sided p-value. After 100,000 draws it is approximately 0.0035. A short run with no extreme draws does not establish zero probability.

Our test statistic is the absolute distance from 50%, measured in percentage points. A result of 56.6% or 43.4% is equally extreme: both are 6.6 points away. The chart folds both directions into one right tail. Shading marks the prechosen rejection region with a false-rejection probability at most 5%; orange bars show the p-value tail beyond the observed statistic. Neither the p-value nor the rejection rule gives the probability that the null is true, a distinction emphasized by the American Statistical Association.

Extra: Why doesn’t every poll get 55%?

Click Draw one poll several times. Each draws different simulated people from the same population. The reported percentages change even though the population’s preference does not. Complete 2,000 polls to build a sampling distribution: the pattern of results across repeated samples.

Change the sample size and repeat. Larger samples generally concentrate more tightly around the true 55%. This is sampling uncertainty, not evidence that campus opinion keeps changing. It also explains why one poll’s majority need not establish a population majority.

What should we learn?

Inference connects a sample to a population through assumptions and repeated-sampling logic. Bootstrap uncertainty and a test against a tie help interpret preferences; they do not determine an official vote’s winner or establish that one funding choice improves outcomes. More simulations cannot repair biased participation. These demos assume independent responses and unbiased sampling.
